Warm-up · from earlier sessions

Two minutes, from memory, before you read on:

You plug in three Access Points inside one conference wing and set all three to 2.4 GHz Channel 3 with 40 MHz wide channels hoping to maximize client speed. Why does Wi-Fi throughput immediately crater worse than a single 20 MHz AP?

In 2.4 GHz, only three channels don't overlap: 1, 6, and 11 (at 20 MHz wide). Picking Channel 3 or bonding 40 MHz overlaps neighboring APs simultaneously, creating destructive Adjacent-Channel Interference (ACI) where radios corrupt each other's frames as unreadable RF noise without cleanly coordinating backoff via CSMA/CA!

1 · Spectrum Physics: 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz & 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E / 7)

BandChannels & WidthPropagation / Interference Profile
2.4 GHzOnly 3 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels: 1, 6, 11Travels farthest through concrete walls; heavily crowded (Bluetooth, microwaves, legacy IoT). Never bond 40 MHz in enterprise!
5 GHzUp to 25 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels (includes DFS weather-radar channels!)Enterprise workhorse (40 MHz sweet spot in offices; 20 MHz in dense stadiums). Must vacate DFS channels immediately for 30 min if radar pulses appear.
6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E/7)Up to 59 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels (clean 80/160/320 MHz blocks)Shortest range (ideal cell containment), zero legacy 802.11b/g slow-client drag, mandates WPA3 and PMF.

Co-Channel Contention (CCI) vs Adjacent-Channel Interference (ACI): Two APs sharing the exact same channel (CCI) share airtime politely using CSMA/CA backoff. Two APs on overlapping channels (ACI, e.g., Ch 1 vs Ch 3) smash into each other's energy at the exact same instant, forcing CRC retries!

2 · CSMA/CA, Half-Duplex Airtime & The Hidden Node Problem

Unlike full-duplex switched fiber Ethernet, radio airwaves are a half-duplex shared medium: only one station (AP or phone) inside a cell can transmit cleanly at one instant.

  • CSMA/CA (Carrier Sense Multiple Access / Collision Avoidance): Every frame is explicitly ACKed at Layer 2! Stations listen before transmitting and draw a random contention backoff window—plus legacy 802.11b/g beacons eat huge airtime slices (why raising minimum basic data rates to 12/24 Mbps dramatically speeds up modern campuses!).
  • Hidden Node & RTS/CTS: Client A on the left edge of an AP hears the AP clearly but cannot hear Client B behind a concrete pillar on the right edge. Both transmit to the AP simultaneously and collide! Enabling RTS/CTS (Request-to-Send / Clear-to-Send) lets the AP broadcast a NAV duration reservation everyone can hear.

3 · Wi-Fi Security & Enterprise Architecture (WPA3, CAPWAP, 802.11k/v/r)

  • WPA2-PSK vs WPA3-SAE vs WPA3-Enterprise: WPA2-PSK's 4-way handshake is vulnerable to offline dictionary cracking if captured. WPA3-SAE (Dragonfly) replaces static PSK hashes with zero-knowledge Password Authenticated Key Exchange, thwarting offline brute-force + granting Forward Secrecy. True enterprise campuses use WPA2/WPA3-Enterprise (802.1X EAP-TLS) authenticating individual user/Android device certs against RADIUS!
  • CAPWAP & Local Switching: Centralized Wireless LAN Controllers (WLC) push config over CAPWAP UDP 5246/5247. In branch/modern designs, FlexConnect / Local Switching bridges user data frames straight onto local switch VLANs so branch web traffic doesn't hairpin across the WAN!
  • Fixing Sticky Android/Laptop Clients (802.11k / 802.11v / 802.11r): Roaming is strictly a client OS decision! Without assistance, phones cling to a distant -78 dBm AP even when standing under a -45 dBm AP. 802.11k gives clients an AP neighbor list (no blind off-channel scans); 802.11v sends BSS Transition hints politely nudging the client to a better AP; 802.11r (Fast BSS Transition) caches pairwise encryption keys so 802.1X roam handoffs complete in <50 ms without dropping VoIP/Zoom calls!
