Packets, and why the network is never instant

Packet switching, the four delays, and the speed-of-light physics of latency

~25 minutesTools: ping★ interview⚡ benchmark
Your win today

You'll be able to estimate the fastest physically possible round trip to any city on Earth, calculate transmission vs propagation delay, and use ping in your terminal as a lie detector to catch servers that cannot be where you assumed they are.

Why this comes first

When a networked application feels slow, the first question an experienced engineer asks is: is this a latency problem or a bandwidth problem?

The two have completely different causes and opposite fixes. Upgrading to a 1 Gbps home internet plan does nothing for an API that makes twenty sequential database queries across an ocean. Conversely, batching queries does nothing if you are downloading a 10 GB file. Every subsequent topic in computer networking—from TCP's 3-way handshake and HTTP/2 multiplexing to CDNs, AWS Transit Gateways, and connection pooling—exists partly to fight one of these two bottlenecks. So we begin with the physics.

Data travels in packets

Your computer never sends "a file", "an email", or "an HTTP request" as one continuous unbroken stream. It chops all data into packets: discrete blocks of bytes, each with a header in front (metadata and addresses, like an envelope) and a payload (a slice of your data).Peterson & Davie call each block "a packet or a message" and use the terms interchangeably at first. Systems Approach §1.2. On a standard Ethernet or Wi-Fi network, a single packet carries at most about 1,500 bytes (the Maximum Transmission Unit, or MTU).

Why chop data up into packets? Because physical communication links are shared. The old telephone network used circuit switching: a phone call reserved a dedicated physical channel end-to-end for its entire duration, even during dead silence. Computer traffic is bursty: a web page requests a burst of data in 200 ms, then the link sits idle for two minutes while you read. Reserving dedicated channels for computer data would waste 99% of global capacity.

Packet switching lets everyone share every link on demand, packet by packet.Circuit vs packet switching: Kurose & Ross §1.3; Systems Approach §1.2.

The trade-off: In packet switching, nobody has guaranteed capacity. If packets arrive at a router faster than its outgoing wire can transmit them, they sit in a hardware memory buffer called a queue. If the queue fills to capacity, any new arriving packet is silently discarded. That is packet loss. The network layer itself does not fix this—recovering lost data is the responsibility of transport protocols like TCP (session 16).

Routers are store-and-forward devices: a router must receive a complete packet before inspecting its destination address and beginning transmission on the next link.

The four delays

At every hop between computers, a packet pays four distinct kinds of delay.The four-component breakdown: Grigorik, High Performance Browser Networking, ch. 1; Kurose & Ross §1.4. Learn these by name; every performance conversation in distributed systems reduces to them.

DelayFormulaWhat it isDepends on
Processing (dproc)—Router reads the packet header, checks for bit errors, and looks up the destination in its routing table.Router CPU/ASIC hardware; typically < 5 microseconds.
Queuing (dqueue)—Time spent waiting in the router's buffer behind other packets for the wire to become free.Current network congestion. Varies wildly from 0 ms to hundreds of ms.
Transmission (dtrans)L / RTime needed to push all bits of the packet onto the physical wire.Packet length L (bits) ÷ Link transmission rate R (bits/sec).
Propagation (dprop)d / sTime needed for a physical signal (light or electrical pulse) to travel along the cable.Physical distance d ÷ Signal propagation speed s.

The #1 beginner trap: Mixing up transmission delay and propagation delay.

  • Transmission delay (L/R) is how long the router takes to push the packet onto the link. It depends on packet size and bandwidth. It does not care whether the link is 1 meter or 10,000 km long.
  • Propagation delay (d/s) is how long a single bit takes to travel through the medium from one end to the other. It depends solely on distance and the speed of light. It does not care how large the packet is or how many gigabits per second the link can push.

Light in optical fiber travels at approximately 2 × 108 m/s (about 2/3 the speed of light in a vacuum).Vacuum: 3.0 × 108 m/s; Copper: ≈ 2.3 × 108 m/s; Fiber: ≈ 2.0 × 108 m/s. Systems Approach §1.5.1. This yields a golden rule of networking worth memorizing:

The Fundamental Law of Fiber Propagation

Fiber optic signals travel at approximately 200 km per millisecond (5 µs per km).

No software engineering, no cloud provider, and no routing protocol can ever beat this speed-of-light physical floor.

Worked Example: The Four Delays in Action

Suppose a server in Chicago transmits a 150 KB payload (L) to an API in Dallas (d ≈ 1,600 km) over a 100 Mbps fiber link (R). Assume zero queuing and 5 µs processing delay.

  • Step 1: Convert units! Always convert bytes to bits: 150 KB × 8 = 1,200,000 bits.
  • Step 2: Calculate Transmission Delay (dtrans):
    1,200,000 bits ÷ 100,000,000 bits/s = 0.012 seconds = 12 ms.
  • Step 3: Calculate Propagation Delay (dprop):
    1,600 km ÷ 200 km/ms = 8 ms (one-way).
  • Step 4: Total one-way latency:
    dproc + dqueue + dtrans + dprop ≈ 0.005 ms + 0 ms + 12 ms + 8 ms = 20.005 ms.

Notice the critical insight: If you upgrade your bandwidth from 100 Mbps to 1 Gbps (10x faster), transmission delay drops from 12 ms down to 1.2 ms. But the propagation delay remains permanently locked at 8 ms. Distance is an inescapable tax.

Code Example: Modeling Delays & BDP in Java and Kotlin

In distributed systems and network client design, engineers compute the Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP) (R × RTT) to size TCP socket receive buffers so pipes stay saturated without dropping packets:

public class NetworkDelays {
    public static final double FIBER_SPEED_KM_PER_MS = 200.0; // 5 µs per km

    // Transmission delay: L (bits) / R (bits/sec) in microseconds
    public static long calcTransmissionDelayMicros(long packetBytes, long linkBps) {
        long packetBits = packetBytes * 8L;
        return (long) Math.round((double) packetBits * 1_000_000.0 / (double) linkBps);
    }

    // Propagation delay: distance / speed in milliseconds
    public static double calcPropagationDelayMillis(double distanceKm) {
        return distanceKm / FIBER_SPEED_KM_PER_MS;
    }

    // Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP): bits in flight = bandwidth (bps) * RTT (sec)
    public static long calcBdpBytes(long linkBps, double rttMillis) {
        double rttSeconds = rttMillis / 1000.0;
        double bdpBits = (double) linkBps * rttSeconds;
        return (long) Math.round(bdpBits / 8.0);
    }

    public static void main(String[] args) {
        long transUs = calcTransmissionDelayMicros(1500, 1_000_000_000L); // 1.5 KB on 1 Gbps
        double propMs = calcPropagationDelayMillis(1200);                  // NYC to Chicago (1200 km)
        long bdpBytes = calcBdpBytes(10_000_000_000L, 70.0);              // 10 Gbps transatlantic (70ms RTT)

        System.out.println("Transmission delay: " + transUs + " µs");
        System.out.println("One-way propagation: " + propMs + " ms");
        System.out.println("Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP): " + (bdpBytes / 1_000_000) + " MB");
    }
}
import kotlin.math.roundToLong

object NetworkDelays {
    const val FIBER_SPEED_KM_PER_MS = 200.0 // 5 µs per km

    // Transmission delay: L (bits) / R (bits/sec) in microseconds
    fun calcTransmissionDelayMicros(packetBytes: Long, linkBps: Long): Long {
        val packetBits = packetBytes * 8L
        return ((packetBits * 1_000_000.0) / linkBps).roundToLong()
    }

    // Propagation delay: distance / speed in milliseconds
    fun calcPropagationDelayMillis(distanceKm: Double): Double =
        distanceKm / FIBER_SPEED_KM_PER_MS

    // Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP): bits in flight = bandwidth (bps) * RTT (sec)
    fun calcBdpBytes(linkBps: Long, rttMillis: Double): Long {
        val rttSeconds = rttMillis / 1000.0
        val bdpBits = linkBps * rttSeconds
        return (bdpBits / 8.0).roundToLong()
    }
}

fun main() {
    val transUs = NetworkDelays.calcTransmissionDelayMicros(1500, 1_000_000_000L)
    val propMs = NetworkDelays.calcPropagationDelayMillis(1200.0)
    val bdpBytes = NetworkDelays.calcBdpBytes(10_000_000_000L, 70.0)

    println("Transmission delay: $transUs µs")
    println("One-way propagation: $propMs ms")
    println("Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP): ${bdpBytes / 1_000_000} MB")
}

Latency, bandwidth, and throughput

  
Picture a physical pipe:

  
    
    
  • Latency is the pipe's length: how long one drop of water (or bit of data) takes to travel from one end to the other. Quoted in milliseconds (ms), usually one-way.
    • 
    
  • Round-Trip Time (RTT) is there and back. Because almost all real-world interactions involve a request followed by a response, communication costs at least one full RTT. ping measures RTT.
    • 
    
  • Bandwidth is the pipe's diameter: the maximum number of bits per second the link can pump.
    • 
    
  • Throughput is the actual rate of data delivered end-to-end. It can never exceed the slowest link along the path (the bottleneck link), and protocol overhead ensures it is always lower than maximum bandwidth.Peterson & Davie note that "bandwidth" and "throughput" are often used interchangeably in loose talk. In engineering, bandwidth is the raw capacity and throughput is what you actually measure.
    • 
  


  
Two unforgettable intuitions

  
    
    
  • The Netflix vs. Zoom Paradox: Streaming 4K Netflix requires high bandwidth (~25 Mbps) but easily tolerates high latency (a 2-second buffer is completely invisible while you watch). Conversely, a live Zoom call or multiplayer game requires tiny bandwidth (~1 Mbps) but completely falls apart if round-trip latency exceeds 150 ms or fluctuates wildly.
    • 
    
  • The Station Wagon full of Hard Drives: Computer scientist Andrew Tanenbaum famously observed: "Never underestimate the bandwidth of a station wagon full of tapes hurtling down the highway." A semi-truck packed with 100 TB SSDs driving across the country has astronomical bandwidth (petabits per second), but horrific latency (3 days). Fiber optics carries tiny payloads per packet, but delivers them in milliseconds.
    • 
  


  
  

    The Classic Interview Trap & Production Reality
    
The Interview Question: "If we upgrade our server's network connection from 10 Mbps to 100 Mbps, will our ping time decrease by 10x?"

    
The Correct Answer: No. A standard ping packet is only 64 bytes. On a 10 Mbps link, transmission takes 0.05 ms; on 100 Mbps, it takes 0.005 ms. A difference of 0.045 ms is completely undetectable inside a 30 ms ping measurement. Ping measures RTT, which is almost entirely governed by propagation delay over physical distance.

    
Production Incident (Bufferbloat): If a router's memory buffer is configured too large, packets wait in queue behind massive file uploads instead of being dropped early. Latency can instantly spike from 15 ms to 900 ms. This phenomenon is known as bufferbloat—the primary cause of audio stutter and gaming lag on shared home or office connections.
