Warm-up · from earlier sessions

Two minutes, from memory, before you read on:

How does BGP establish neighbor sessions, and what does the AS-PATH attribute contain?

BGP establishes point-to-point TCP sessions on destination port 179. The AS-PATH attribute contains an ordered list of every Autonomous System Number (ASN) that the advertised IP prefix has traversed.

Routing by economics, not physical distance

Interior routing protocols (like OSPF) always choose the shortest physical or highest-bandwidth path. BGP does not.See Geoff Huston, BGP: Building Reliable Networks with the Border Gateway Protocol.

On the global Internet, routing decisions are governed by dollars and business contracts. Sending a terabyte of traffic across an expensive Tier-1 transit provider costs money; sending that same traffic across a direct cross-connect at an Internet Exchange Point (IXP) is free. BGP exists to enforce these economic policies.

Transit vs Settlement-Free Peering

Autonomous Systems enter into two primary business relationships:

  • IP Transit (Customer → Provider): A customer network pays a large upstream ISP (e.g. Lumen, Telia/Arelion, Cogent) for access to the entire global Internet routing table. The provider charges based on committed data rates or 95th-percentile burst bandwidth.
  • Settlement-Free Peering: Two networks (e.g. Netflix and Comcast, or Google and a regional fiber ISP) connect directly at an Internet Exchange Point (IXP). Neither pays the other a cent! They agree to exchange traffic destined strictly and solely for each other's customers.

The Gao-Rexford Principle (No Free Rides!)

To avoid bankruptcy, networks follow the valley-free routing rule:L. Gao & J. Rexford, "Stable Internet Routing Without Global Coordination" (IEEE/ACM Transactions on Networking).

  • You advertise your own prefixes and your paying customers' prefixes to everyone.
  • You never advertise routes learned from Peer A to Peer B or to an upstream Transit provider! If you did, Peer A would route all its traffic through your internal backbone to reach Peer B, forcing you to pay for their data transit.

The BGP Best-Path Selection Algorithm

When a BGP router receives multiple route advertisements for the exact same IP prefix from different peers, it evaluates them against a strict, deterministic tie-breaker gauntlet:

Steering Traffic: Inbound vs Outbound

Network engineers have different controls for outbound vs inbound traffic:

  • Steering Outbound Traffic (Easy): You have total control over what leaves your network. By setting LOCAL_PREF on incoming BGP updates from peers, you force all your internal routers to prefer your settlement-free peering links over expensive transit links.
  • Steering Inbound Traffic (Hard): You cannot dictate how external networks configure their local preferences. However, you can make your secondary/backup transit link look unappealing by using AS-PATH Prepending: repeating your own ASN multiple times in the advertisement (e.g. AS-PATH: 100 100 100). External routers evaluating rule #4 (shortest AS-PATH) will avoid the long prepended path in favor of your primary link!

Hands-on: Analyzing BGP path selection

  1. Inspect the full list of candidate paths for a destination prefix: 
    router# show ip bgp 198.51.100.0/24
    Notice the * (valid route) next to all received paths, but only one line has > (the elected best path!).
  2. Inspect the attributes of the winning path: 
    router# show ip bgp 198.51.100.0/24 | include (metric|locprf|path)
    Verify that the route with locprf 200 won over the alternate route with locprf 100.
  3. Configure an outbound route-map to prepend your ASN to backup transit provider: 
    router(config-route-map)# set as-path prepend 65001 65001 65001
    Confirm via Looking Glass that external networks now see a 4-hop AS-PATH and divert inbound traffic away.

Free Recall Practice

From memory: why does Local Preference take precedence over AS-PATH length in BGP path selection? How does Hot-Potato routing work?

Key points to compare against:

  • Local Preference precedence: Local Preference (Rule #2) is evaluated before AS-PATH length (Rule #4) so an Autonomous System can enforce business policies—prioritizing free peering or cheap links over shorter but financially expensive transit paths.
  • Hot-Potato routing: When all higher BGP attributes are equal, Rule #8 selects the exit router with the lowest internal IGP metric (cost). This hands the packet off to an external network as quickly as possible, minimizing internal backbone resource usage.
