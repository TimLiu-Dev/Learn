Warm-up · from earlier sessions

Two minutes, from memory, before you read on:

If Router A receives a route for 192.0.2.0/24 and another for 192.0.2.0/25, which route forwards traffic to 192.0.2.1?

The router forwards traffic matching 192.0.2.0/25 because of Longest Prefix Match (LPM). More specific subnet masks (/25 beats /24) always override broader prefixes, regardless of AS-PATH length.

The Original Sin of BGP

When BGP was drafted on three mustard-stained napkins in 1989, the Internet was a cooperative academic network of trusted universities and military labs.RFC 6480: An Infrastructure to Support Secure Internet Routing. M. Lepinski, S. Kent (2012).

Because BGP was designed without cryptographic identity verification, it operates on blind faith: if any router in the world advertises that it owns an IP block, upstream routers accept the announcement and broadcast it to the planet. This vulnerability manifests in two primary threats:

1. BGP Route Hijacking

In a BGP Hijack, a rogue or misconfigured Autonomous System announces an IP prefix it does not own. Because of Longest Prefix Match (LPM), the attack is devastatingly simple:

Case Study: The 2008 YouTube Pakistan Outage

In February 2008, the Pakistan Telecommunication Authority ordered local ISP PTCL (AS17557) to block YouTube within the country.RIPE NCC Post-Mortem: Pakistan Telecom BGP Routing Incident.

  • To blackhole YouTube locally, PTCL injected a /24 route (208.65.153.0/24) into its internal routers, pointing to null.
  • However, PTCL accidentally leaked this /24 route to its upstream transit provider (PCCW).
  • YouTube's legitimate announcement was a broader /22 block (208.65.152.0/22). By the laws of Longest Prefix Match, /24 trumps /22 everywhere on Earth! Within 20 minutes, PCCW flooded the fake route globally, redirecting nearly all global YouTube visitors into Pakistan, completely collapsing PTCL's links and taking YouTube offline worldwide for two hours.

2. BGP Route Leaks

A Route Leak occurs when an Autonomous System learns prefixes from Transit Provider A and accidentally re-advertises them to Transit Provider B (violating the Gao-Rexford rule). Suddenly, millions of packets between Europe and Asia attempt to route through a tiny 1 Gbps regional ISP in the Midwest, creating massive brownouts.

The Solution: RPKI & Cryptographic Route Validation

To eliminate BGP hijacking, the Internet engineering community developed RPKI (Resource Public Key Infrastructure). RPKI ties Regional Internet Registry (RIR) address allocations to cryptographic public key certificates.

Route Origin Authorizations (ROAs)

The legitimate owner of an IP block generates a cryptographically signed object called a ROA (Route Origin Authorization):

Prefix: 203.0.113.0/24
Max Length: /24
Authorized ASN: AS65001

The ROA explicitly declares: "Only AS65001 is legally authorized to announce 203.0.113.0 up to a maximum specificity of /24."

Route Origin Validation (ROV) States

Border routers download verified ROAs from public RPKI caches (via the RTR protocol) and evaluate every incoming BGP announcement into three states:

  • 1. Valid: The announced prefix matches a valid ROA, the prefix length is ≤ Max Length, and the origin ASN matches the authorized ASN. The route is accepted with full confidence!
  • 2. Invalid: A ROA exists for this prefix, but either the announcing ASN is wrong OR the prefix length exceeds Max Length (e.g. announcing a /25 when Max Length is /24). The router immediately DROPS the route! Hijacks are thwarted in hardware.
  • 3. NotFound / Unknown: No ROA has been signed for this prefix yet. The route is accepted as standard unauthenticated BGP (backward compatibility).

Free Recall Practice

From memory: why does announcing a /24 hijack traffic from a /22, and how does RPKI Route Origin Validation prevent this?

Key points to compare against:

  • Why /24 hijacks /22: Longest Prefix Match (LPM) dictates that routers must always forward packets according to the most specific subnet mask. Even if the legitimate /22 has a shorter AS-PATH, routers forward to the hijacker's /24 announcement.
  • How RPKI stops it: The legitimate owner signs a ROA specifying their exact authorized ASN and Max Length (e.g. /22). When the hijacker announces a unauthorized ASN or a /24 exceeding Max Length, RPKI-enabled routers classify the route as Invalid and discard it immediately.
