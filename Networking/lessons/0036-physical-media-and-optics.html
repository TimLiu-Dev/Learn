Warm-up · from earlier sessions

Two minutes, from memory, before you read on:

What physical speed limit governs propagation delay through copper wire and fiber optic cables?

Signals in copper wire and optical fiber travel at the speed of light in the dielectric medium (~200,000 km/s, or approximately 2/3 the speed of light in a vacuum: ~5 microseconds per kilometer).

Why physical media matters to network engineers

Every bit in every packet must ultimately traverse a physical waveguide—either pulses of electrical voltage across copper twisted-pair conductors or photons fired down strands of high-purity silica glass.See Peterson & Davie §1.3: Perspectives on Connecting Hardware.

Software engineers often treat the physical layer as an abstract pipe, but in enterprise networks and data centers, physical layer faults cause the most confounding outages: intermittent CRC errors, link flapping under temperature changes, and asymmetric routing bottlenecks caused by bad link aggregation hashing.

Optical Transceivers & Form Factors

Modern enterprise switches do not ship with hardwired fiber ports; instead, they contain modular cages into which engineers insert hot-swappable transceivers:

  • SFP (Small Form-factor Pluggable): 1 Gbps (1000BASE-SX / LX).
  • SFP+ (Enhanced SFP): 10 Gbps (10GBASE-SR / LR). Same physical size as 1G SFP.
  • SFP28: 25 Gbps down a single electrical lane (common in modern server NICs).
  • QSFP28 (Quad SFP28): 100 Gbps (uses 4 parallel 25G channels; can be broken out into four 25G SFP28 links).

Calculating the Optical Power Budget

When connecting two optical transceivers over a fiber run, the link only functions if the optical signal reaching the receiver falls between the receiver's Sensitivity and its Saturation limit:

Power Loss (dB) = (Distance in km × Cable Loss/km) + (Connectors × 0.5 dB) + (Splices × 0.1 dB)

Received Power (dBm) = Transmit Power (dBm) - Total Power Loss (dB)

If received power is weaker than sensitivity (e.g. -14 dBm received when sensitivity is -11 dBm), the link suffers bit errors. If received power is stronger than saturation (e.g. firing an 80 km optic across a 2-meter patch cord), the photo-detector is permanently fried!

Link Aggregation (LACP / IEEE 802.3ad)

To scale bandwidth beyond a single port (e.g., bonding two 10G links into a 20G trunk) while providing automatic failover, switches use LACP (Link Aggregation Control Protocol):

  • Combines up to 8 active physical interfaces into a single logical LAG (Link Aggregation Group) or port-channel.
  • The Packet Reordering Paradox: LACP never round-robins individual packets across links, because TCP connections collapse if packets arrive out of order! Instead, switches calculate a hash of the packet's headers (typically the 5-tuple: src/dst IP + src/dst port + protocol) to pin each TCP stream to a single physical member.

Hands-on: Checking optical levels and LACP status

  1. On an enterprise switch (or simulation), view DOM (Digital Optical Monitoring) transceivers: 
    switch# show interfaces transceiver details
    Look for: Tx Power: -2.4 dBm, Rx Power: -8.1 dBm, and Optical Link State: OK.
  2. Inspect an active Link Aggregation Group (LAG): 
    switch# show lacp neighbor
    Verify that both member interfaces (e.g. Eth1/1, Eth1/2) are in the AL (Active / LACP partner synchronized) state.
  3. Test how single-stream TCP behaves across a 2-port LAG: 
    $ iperf3 -c 10.0.0.2 -P 1
    Notice that a single stream caps out at exactly 10 Gbps—never 20 Gbps—due to 5-tuple flow pinning!

Free Recall Practice

From memory: why does Multi-Mode fiber have a much shorter distance limit than Single-Mode fiber? How does LACP prevent TCP packet reordering?

Key points to compare against:

  • Multi-Mode limit: Multi-mode fiber has a wide 50 µm core that allows light to travel along multiple concurrent reflective paths (modes). Because diagonal paths travel longer distances than straight axial paths, pulses spread out over time (modal dispersion) and overlap, corrupting data beyond a few hundred meters.
  • LACP packet order: LACP hashes packet headers (usually the 5-tuple: src/dst IP and src/dst port) so that all packets belonging to a specific TCP connection always traverse the exact same physical link in the bundle.
