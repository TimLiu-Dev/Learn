Track 07: Android Automotive OS (AAOS) • Lesson 0031

Functional Safety (ISO 26262):
ASIL Decomposition, FFI & Safety Islands

When an Android phone crashes, the user sees an "App isn't responding" dialog. When automotive software crashes at 70 mph on a highway, human lives are on the line. In this lesson, you will master ISO 26262, the international standard for automotive functional safety, ASIL classifications (QM through ASIL-D), Freedom From Interference (FFI), and hardware safety islands.

Mental Model Shift: Consumer "Move Fast" vs Automotive Safety-Critical

In consumer software, bugs are patched in weekly CI/CD sprints. If an app runs out of memory or drops a UI frame, the runtime garbage collector reclaims heap memory and the user reloads.

In Automotive Functional Safety (ISO 26262), software faults can cause loss of vehicle control. System behavior is strictly partitioned: Quality Management (QM) code (like Android Spotify or Google Maps) is physically isolated from ASIL-D systems (like steering, braking, or airbag deployment) so an Android crash cannot compromise vehicle control!

1. Hazard Analysis and Risk Assessment (HARA)

ISO 26262 determines an Automotive Safety Integrity Level (ASIL) based on three fundamental parameters evaluated during hazard analysis:

  • Severity (S): Potential harm to driver, passengers, and pedestrians.
    • S0: No injuries.
    • S1: Light to moderate injuries.
    • S2: Severe to life-threatening injuries (survival probable).
    • S3: Life-threatening to fatal injuries (survival uncertain).
  • Exposure (E): Probability of the operational situation occurring during vehicle life.
    • E1: Very low (<1% of driving time).
    • E2: Low (1%–10% of driving time).
    • E3: Medium (10%–20% of driving time).
    • E4: High (>20% of driving time; e.g. driving on a highway).
  • Controllability (C): Likelihood that the driver or bystanders can avoid the accident.
    • C1: Simply controllable (>99% of drivers can avoid harm).
    • C2: Normally controllable (90%–99% of drivers can avoid harm).
    • C3: Difficult to control or uncontrollable (<90% can avoid harm).

2. Freedom From Interference (FFI)

Why isn't Android Automotive OS certified to ASIL-D? Because Android contains tens of millions of lines of code, a complex Linux kernel, dynamic heap allocation, and continuous garbage collection. Verifying all execution paths to ASIL-D standards is mathematically and practically impossible.

Therefore, automotive systems apply Freedom From Interference (FFI) to ensure that QM software (Android) cannot adversely impact ASIL software:

  • Spatial Isolation: Memory Protection Units (MPU) or Hardware MMUs prevent Android from reading or overwriting safety-critical memory regions.
  • Temporal Isolation: Hardware watchdog timers and real-time RTOS schedulers ensure safety tasks always meet their hard deadlines regardless of Android CPU load.
  • Communication Isolation: End-to-End (E2E) protection protocols add sequence counters and CRCs to bus frames so corrupted or delayed messages are discarded instantly.

Windowed Watchdogs: Anti-Babbling Protection

A standard watchdog resets if it isn't kicked before a timeout. But what if a buggy loop spins wildly and kicks the watchdog continuously?

A Windowed Watchdog requires kicks within an exact temporal window: kicking too late or kicking too early triggers a hardware fault reset!
